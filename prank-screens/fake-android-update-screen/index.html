Pick the screen you want, tap start, and the page fills the display: the progress creeps forward, stalls, restarts and begins all over again. Hand the phone to a friend and watch them wait for an update that will never finish. Nothing is downloaded or installed, no setting changes, and a swipe back ends it instantly.

What the fake Android update screen does

A picture of an update screen gives itself away in seconds, because nothing moves. This one is live. The progress bar advances in real time, pauses at the points where real updates tend to pause, and keeps going just long enough for the person holding the phone to believe it. When it reaches the end, the phone appears to restart, shows the familiar boot messages, and then the update starts again from zero. There is no cancel button and no menu to escape into, so the only sensible move for your victim is to wait — or to hand the phone back looking worried.

Everything runs inside a single browser tab. The fake Android update screen does not need an app, asks for no permissions, and cannot touch the operating system, your files or your settings. The percentage on screen is just a number counting up inside the page.

Two update screens: Classic and Modern

The fake Android update screen comes in two styles, one for the update everybody remembers and one for the update a current phone actually shows.

  • Classic — the robot installer. A black screen with the green Android robot standing in the middle, its chest panel swung open and a blue faceted shape spinning inside. Under it sits the line “Installing system update…” and a thin white progress bar. When the bar fills, the phone appears to reboot into the “Android is upgrading…” dialog, which counts through the apps it is optimizing one at a time — “Optimizing app 42 of 151” — before the whole thing starts again. This is the screen most people picture when they hear “Android update”, and it is the default.
  • Modern — the Settings update page. The system update page that current phones show while they update: a large “Installing system update…” headline, a short line underneath (“Optimizing your device, this may take a while”), the new wavy progress line that ripples as it fills, and a note about the Android version being installed. After it completes, the screen shows “Restarting…”, goes black, and comes back with “Phone is starting…” — then the update begins again. It even draws its own status bar with the real time, because a fullscreen browser hides the phone’s own.

The Classic style also has an optional ending. Tick End on the “No command” error and, instead of looping, the installer reaches 100%, the screen goes dark, and the robot reappears lying on its back with a red warning triangle and the words “No command.” It is the screen people associate with a phone that will not boot, and it stays there until you exit.

How to set up the prank on an Android phone

  1. Open this page in Chrome on the phone you want to use.
  2. Choose Classic or Modern. For Modern, pick the Android version and match the phone’s light or dark theme.
  3. Choose a starting point. 0% shows the whole sequence; 37% or 73% opens mid-install, which is more believable if the phone “was updating when you picked it up”.
  4. Choose a speed. Slow takes about three minutes per cycle, normal about one minute, and fast about twenty-five seconds.
  5. Tap Start Fake Update. Chrome goes fullscreen and hides its address bar and the status bar, so the fake Android update screen covers the whole display.
  6. Hand the phone over, or leave it face up on the table where someone will notice it.

To stop, swipe back or press the back button, tap the invisible spot in the top-right corner, or close the tab. Taps anywhere else are ignored, exactly as they are on a real update screen.

Which style fits your phone

Every brand puts its own spin on updates, but almost all of them now install inside the Settings app and then restart. The fake Android update screen works on any Android phone and in any modern browser; the notes below are about which of the two styles will look most natural on each brand.

  • Samsung Galaxy. Many Galaxy A-series phones still restart into a black, fullscreen installer with a thin progress bar and a percentage, so the Classic style’s black screen and thin bar will feel familiar to Galaxy owners.
  • Google Pixel. Pixels install in the background and show the system update page in Settings. The Modern style follows that page closely, so it is the obvious choice for a Pixel.
  • Xiaomi, Redmi and POCO. Older MIUI phones restarted into a dark screen with a large percentage counter; newer HyperOS phones update inside the Updater app. Use Classic for an older phone and Modern for a newer one.
  • OPPO, OnePlus and realme. All three update inside Settings with a progress button that fills as the update installs, then restart. Modern blends in best.
  • vivo and iQOO. These phones install in Settings and then spend a while on a dark “optimizing” screen after the restart. Either style works; Classic’s “Optimizing app” dialog lands particularly well.
  • Motorola. Motorola shows its own “Updating…” page with a percentage in Settings, then restarts. Choose Modern.
  • Huawei and Honor. Both update from a Settings page and restart with a logo screen. Modern is the closer match.
  • Tecno, Infinix and itel. These share one system update app that installs in Settings and finishes with a restart. Modern again.

If you are not sure, pick Classic. Whatever brand someone owns, they know the green robot means “my phone is busy updating”, and that is all the prank needs.

Android 14, 15, 16 and 17

The Modern style of the fake Android update screen mentions the version being installed — “…along with Android 16”, for example. Pick the version that makes sense for the phone in your hand: one number ahead of what it currently runs is the most believable, because that is exactly what a real update would be. If the phone is already on the newest release, choose that same number; people rarely remember which version they are on.

Why the screen does not switch off

Most phones dim and lock after thirty seconds without a touch, which would kill a prank that depends on nobody touching the phone. While the fake Android update screen is running, the page asks the browser to keep the screen awake, the same way a video player does. The request ends the moment you exit, and your normal screen timeout comes straight back. Older browsers that do not support this may still dim on their usual timer, so set a longer timeout if you are using one.

Tips for a convincing fake Android update screen

  • Use slow speed. Real updates take a long time, and a bar that races to the end looks fake. Slow gives you three minutes of believable progress per cycle.
  • Start part-way through. “It was already updating when I picked it up” is a better story than watching it begin. Start at 37% or 73%.
  • Match the theme. If the phone normally runs in dark mode, use the dark theme, and vice versa.
  • Turn the brightness up. A dim black screen reads as “off”; a bright one reads as “busy”.
  • Save “No command” for round two. Once the first prank has been revealed, tick the error ending and start again at 73%. Someone who has just been fooled once rarely expects a second, scarier screen.

Is the fake Android update screen safe?

Completely. It is a web page made of HTML, CSS and JavaScript. It cannot install anything, cannot restart the phone and cannot change a single setting — the “restart” you see is a black screen drawn by the page. There is no download, no sign-up and nothing to remove afterwards. Close the tab and the phone is exactly as it was.

How to tell a real update from the fake one

If someone has handed you a phone that seems to be updating and you suspect a trick, swipe back or press the back button. A real update screen ignores you, but the fake Android update screen closes and drops you straight back into the browser. Opening the recent-apps view is another giveaway: the “update” turns out to be a browser tab. Real updates also never ask you to visit a website to finish installing, which is worth remembering outside of pranks too: a pop-up on a web page that claims your phone needs an urgent update is an advert or a scam, never a genuine Android update.

Record it for a short video

The reaction is the best part, so many people film it. Prop the phone up with the fake Android update screen running at slow speed, start recording, and let your friend discover it. For a clean clip of the screen on its own, use the phone’s built-in screen recorder after launching the prank, or film a second phone running it. The Classic robot installer reads instantly in a vertical video, and the “No command” ending makes a natural punchline.

Prank responsibly

A fake Android update screen that never finishes is a gentle joke, but timing matters. Do not use it on someone who is about to leave the house, waiting on an important call, or relying on their phone for directions. The “No command” ending in particular can make people think their phone is broken, so stay close and end the joke while it is still funny. The fastest reveal is a swipe back — the update closes and the browser page reappears.
