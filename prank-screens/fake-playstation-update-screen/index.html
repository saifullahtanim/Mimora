This free fake PlayStation update screen recreates the PS5 system software updater in your browser. It runs through preparation, a restart, and installation, with a moving percentage and the familiar power warning below the progress bar. After reaching 100%, it briefly goes black and begins another cycle. Nothing is installed, no console is contacted, and ESC ends it instantly.

What the fake PlayStation update screen shows

The screen uses the PS5 layout: “System Software Update” in the top-left corner, light text, a thin horizontal progress bar, and a dark background with subtle particles during installation. The percentage sits above the bar, and “Don’t turn off your PS5 during installation.” appears underneath with the crossed-circle symbol.

The preview and fullscreen animation use the same layout. On a television or monitor, the text and bar keep their relative positions as the screen grows. On a phone, spacing adjusts so the warning stays readable. The visual reference is the PS5 updater; the speed, pauses and repeated cycle are part of the browser simulation.

Preparation, restart and installation

Starting from 0% opens the preparation screen. It explains that the PS5 will restart once preparation is complete, then shows “Preparing to install…” beside the progress percentage. After preparation, the animation displays “Restarting your PS5…” and the power-cord warning.

A brief black screen leads into “Please wait…”, followed by the installation screen. Here “Installing…” appears above the percentage. Installation advances to 100%, holds briefly, then the screen goes black before the next cycle. The fake PlayStation update continues until you exit.

The sequence follows a normal update rather than displaying a simulated game crash. It does not show CE-34878-0 or pretend an application has failed. Preparation and installation have separate progress percentages, so the installation stage begins from zero after the first restart.

Why the restart sells it

The restart in the middle is what makes this fake PlayStation update believable. Anyone watching a progress bar reach 100% relaxes, because in their experience that means the wait is over. Here the first 100% only finishes preparation. The screen announces a restart, goes black, says “Please wait…” and then starts a second bar from zero under “Installing…”. A real PS5 update also runs in two stages like this, so it reads as a normal update rather than a trick.

The loop is the punchline. When installation reaches 100%, the screen holds for a moment, goes black as though the console is booting into the new software, and then comes back with “Preparing to install…” at 0%. Nobody expects a second update straight after the first, and the moment preparation begins again is usually when the room works out what is going on.

PS5 and PS4 use different update screens

This page reproduces the PS5 system software update screen. It is not a shared PS4-and-PS5 skin: the PS4 interface has different colours, layout and wording. If you want the prank to match a PS5 setup, connect the browser to the same television or monitor that normally displays the console.

Choose where the update starts

Choose 0% to see the complete sequence, including preparation and the first restart. Choose 37% or 73% to open directly on the installation screen at that percentage. These options are useful when you want the display to look as though an update is already underway.

The starting percentage applies to the first launch only. Once installation finishes, the fake PlayStation update returns to preparation and runs its full cycle. Leaving and launching again uses the progress option you selected in the settings panel.

Adjust the speed

Slow runs a complete cycle in about three minutes. Normal takes about one minute, and Fast takes about 25 seconds. Starting part-way through installation shortens the first cycle. Subsequent cycles include the full preparation and installation sequence.

The progress deliberately pauses at a few points before moving again. Those pauses imitate an update processing files, but they are not fixed checkpoints in real PS5 firmware. Real installation time depends on the console and the update. Here the selected speed controls how long the animation takes.

A worked example of the fake PlayStation update

The setup that works best is simple. Someone is about to sit down for an evening of gaming. Before they reach the TV, set Starting progress to 73%, and set Update speed to Slow if you plan to leave the room or Normal if you want to stay and watch. Launch it, then hand over the controller as if nothing is wrong.

The 73% opening is worth the extra second it takes to set. Starting at zero tells someone they have time to kill, so they wander off and miss the joke. Starting at 73% tells them the install is nearly finished, so they sit and wait. Then they watch the fake PlayStation update reach 100%, go black, and start preparing all over again.

Leave the button-press warning off if you want the screen to ignore them completely. Switch it on if you would rather every key press or tap bring up the power warning for a couple of seconds. The controller does nothing either way, because the console is not connected to the page, and that silence is part of the joke.

How to run the fake PlayStation update on a television

  1. Open this page on a laptop or another device with a browser, and connect it to the TV if needed.
  2. Choose the starting progress and update speed.
  3. Enable the optional button-press warning if you want clicks and keys to display the power warning.
  4. Press Start Fake Update to launch the fullscreen screen.
  5. Press ESC or click or tap the invisible top-right exit area when you are finished.

The browser requests fullscreen when you launch. If your browser does not allow fullscreen, the animation still fills its available window. The exit area in the top-right corner stays invisible, including when you move the mouse. Click or tap that corner to exit. The screen never locks you into an update.

For the most convincing result, connect the laptop to the television over HDMI and launch the prank before you switch the TV to that input, so nobody ever sees a browser. Once it is running there is no address bar, no tab strip and no exit button on screen. Nobody checks which input a TV is set to when it shows exactly what they expect, which is why a fake PlayStation update on the living-room television survives scrutiny that the same page on a laptop would not.

Clicks, keys and the optional warning

With the warning switched off, ordinary clicks and keys leave the animation running. Turn on Warn when they press anything to show the power warning when someone interacts with the screen. This extra message is optional and starts off disabled.

ESC and the invisible top-right exit area work in every phase, including the black restart screens. Closing the browser tab also ends the fake PlayStation update immediately. Exiting cancels the animation so it does not continue advancing behind the settings panel.

Why this one lands so hard on PlayStation owners

PlayStation consoles download updates quietly in rest mode, so finding one waiting to install is completely normal. Nobody is surprised when a PS5 wants to update before a game. That familiarity is the prank’s best friend: a fake PlayStation update does not ask anyone to believe in an unusual event, only a tedious one they have sat through many times.

It also taps something more uncomfortable. PlayStation owners know that an update interrupted at the wrong moment can be genuinely bad news, and stories about safe mode and rebuilding the database are widely shared, even among people who have never had to do either. So “Don’t turn off your PS5 during installation” is not read as boilerplate. It is read as a real instruction, which is why nobody reaches for the power button to make the problem go away. They wait, and the longer they wait, the better the restart lands.

Nothing on your console changes

This is a browser animation made with HTML, CSS and JavaScript. It does not connect to a PlayStation, download a firmware file, sign into an account, or change games and save data. The progress bar and percentages are drawn inside the page. The restart is a visual transition, not a restart of your device.

How long should you let it run?

Shorter than you think. One full cycle of the fake PlayStation update is usually the sweet spot: the install finishes, the screen goes black, and preparation begins again. On Normal that takes about a minute, which is long enough for the second “Preparing to install…” to land. Slow stretches the cycle to about three minutes and suits a prank you set up before leaving the room.

Let it loop twice and the joke stops being a surprise and starts being someone’s actual evening. Show them ESC as soon as the restart has landed and it stays funny.

When to leave this one alone

It is one of the gentler pranks on the site. Nobody believes their console is broken, only that it has chosen the worst possible moment to update, which every player has lived through. That shared experience is why it works, and also why it is worth a little judgement about timing.

Do not run a fake PlayStation update on someone about to start a ranked match, join friends who are already waiting, or begin a session they have been looking forward to all week. Keep it to one short cycle and show them the exit control once the joke has landed.
